The Clinician’s AI Safety Check

A brief pause before using AI at work.

Before you open the tool

The quick check

Three questions. Any answer that needs action tells you what to do.

Three questions

1. Tool and task

Is this specific AI tool, account and intended use permitted by my organisation?

Permission is specific. A tool approved for one task, or through one account, isn’t approved for everything.

No: stop. Not sure: ask first.

2. Information

Am I only using information permitted for this tool and workflow?

Include attachments, screenshots and anything you might paste in later.

No: change your approach, or stop. Not sure: ask first.

3. Identifiability

Could these details, alone or together, identify a person?

Removing a name isn’t enough. Age, condition, place and circumstances can add up. See how.

Yes or not sure: pause and follow local guidance.

Why removing a name isn’t enough

The details add up

Each detail on its own fits a lot of people. Watch what happens when they overlap.

Three fictional details

  • In their eighties
  • Admitted after a fall this week
  • Lives above the only shop on their street

Each detail on its own fits lots of people. Together, they may leave just one. None of them is a name.

These details are invented for this lesson. There’s no safe number of details: what identifies someone depends on context and on what else is known.

Read once, keep for reference

The full guide

Nine checks across three stages. You don’t need to read all of this every time. The quick check is for that.

BeforeBefore you open the tool

Check 1. Where: approval

Is this exact tool, account and environment approved for my intended task?

Approval is specific. The same tool can be approved through a Trust account and not a personal one, or for drafting and not for clinical decisions. If you can’t point to the permission, assume you don’t have it.

Who can tell you

Check 2. What: information

What am I entering, and is every data type and detail permitted and necessary?

Go through what you’re about to enter, one type at a time: text, files, images, numbers. Leave out anything the tool doesn’t need to do the task. Often the task doesn’t need the person at all.

Check 3. Why: purpose

Is the purpose appropriate and authorised, and could details identify someone alone or together?

Think about what you’re asking AI to do. Drafting a general leaflet is different from helping make a decision about one patient. Then check whether the details could identify someone when put together.

The details add up
DuringWhile you’re using it

Check 4. Minimise

Am I staying within permitted information boundaries, including attachments, screenshots and follow-up prompts?

AI tools ask follow-up questions, and each one invites a little more detail. This is prompt creep. Try it:

Practice chat. Not a real toolFictional
YouCan you draft a simple home exercise programme for someone recovering after a fall?
AI toolHappy to help. To tailor it, can you tell me the patient’s full name, exact age and which ward they’re on?

Check 5. Boundaries

Am I keeping AI within its authorised supporting role and retaining human clinical judgement?

Drafting support is not clinical decision support. If the output starts recommending what to do for a specific patient, you’ve moved into a different kind of use, which may need different approval.

Check 6. Reassess

If the task changes, AI asks for more data or a concern arises, should I stop and seek advice?

The check you did at the start covered the task you started with. If the task grows, run the quick check again.

AI confidence is not evidence.

AfterBefore you save, share or use the output

Check 7. What changed?

Did AI invent, omit, distort or overstate anything clinically important compared with the source?

Errors aren’t only invented facts. Look for what’s missing, what’s been softened or strengthened, and what now means something slightly different.

Check 8. Verify

Have I checked relevant facts, figures, medications, links, evidence and references against suitable sources?

Open every link. Find every reference. Check every dose and number against a source you’d trust without AI.

Can you spot an invented reference?

Check 9. Approve and handle

Has the output followed the appropriate professional review, patient-specific or wider-distribution route, and storage and versioning rules?

Where the output goes decides the route. It’s one route or the other, not both:

Where is it going?

Route A

For one patient you’re treating

Review it clinically, personalise it inside approved systems, and record it according to local records rules.

Route B

Reused, displayed or shared

Leaflets, posters, templates, service information. These follow your organisation’s patient information, accessibility and communications processes before use.

Either way: date it, give it a version, and store it where your organisation says it belongs.

One procedure, used everywhere

If something goes wrong

If you’ve shared something you shouldn’t have, or relied on an output that may be unsafe:

  1. 1Stop

    Stop further disclosure, sharing or reliance on potentially unsafe output.

  2. 2Report

    Promptly use your organisation’s approved incident channel. Contact the relevant manager, IG or data protection, security or clinical safety team as required.

  3. 3Record

    Document the facts, time, tool and account, possible exposure and any patient impact, only in approved systems.

  4. 4Contain

    Follow instructions about access, retention, deletion and evidence preservation. Don’t assume deleting the chat reverses the disclosure.

  5. 5Cooperate

    Follow the response and any patient-safety actions. Your organisation decides on any regulatory notifications.

An AI-generated clinical error may need clinical safety escalation even when no data breach has happened.

Before you need them

Who to ask

Most people find out who these people are when something has already gone wrong. Find out now.

Your line manager

Where to start. They can tell you local practice and who the right specialist is.

IG or data protection

What information can go into which tool, and what to do after a disclosure.

IT or security

Which tools and accounts are approved, and how they’re set up.

AI governance

If your organisation has one: approved uses and how new uses are assessed.

Clinical safety officer

Clinical risk from digital tools, including errors in AI output.

Questions worth asking

  • Which AI tools and accounts are approved here, and for which tasks?
  • What information am I allowed to enter into each one?
  • Who approves a new use of an approved tool?
  • How do I report an AI-related mistake, including a clinical error with no data breach?
  • Where should AI-assisted outputs and templates be stored?
